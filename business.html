Eli Lilly’s policy requiring 340B hospitals submit claims-level data in order to receive statutory drug discounts garnered a fresh wave of pushback this week from Missouri’s attorney general and a cadre of Massachusetts hospitals, joining others who have described the requirements as unlawful.

Lilly announced its controversial policy for 340B dispensations in January and first put it into effect for a subset of hospitals in June. It applies to all pharmacy and medical dispensations, including in-house pharmacy and contract pharmacy dispenses, in most states.

The company said doing so would allow it to spot instances when a drug purchase is improperly granted multiple discounts under overlapping programs. Hospitals have contended that the extra scrutiny is unnecessary and introduces substantial administrative burden.

Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway has come in on the side of the opposition. On Monday, she issued a cease-and-desist letter to Lilly, writing that the policy violates the Missouri Merchandising Practices Act, a consumer protection law. If the drugmaker doesn’t end its requirement for Missouri providers, the policy “may result in subsequent legal action,” including an injunction and monetary civil penalties, the letter reads.

“Big Pharma does not get to bully Missouri hospitals or hold prescription drug prices hostage,” Hanaway said in an accompanying release. “Eli Lilly is abusing its market power to impose higher prescription prices in Missouri. These tactics fall hardest on rural communities that depend on the 340B program to keep clinics open and ensure patients receive the care they need.”

Hanaway’s letter and release highlight Lily’s decision to end discounts for University of Missouri Health Care in July. The system later agreed to provide the data as requested, but “now bears the heavy costs and burdens of complying with Lilly’s requirements,” the attorney general’s office wrote.

A day later, six Massachusetts hospitals hit Lilly with a new lawsuit to block the policy. Massachusetts General Hospital, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Salem Hospital, UMass Memorial Medical Center and Umass Memorial Health — Harrington Hospital filed the lawsuit.

Their complaint filed in federal court describes “corporate extortion” from “the largest pharmaceutical company in the world” that “deprives them of resources essential to serving their most vulnerable patients.”

The hospitals “rightfully refused to comply” with the policy and had their 340B discounts pulled, echoing the claimed injuries from others who have sued over the policy, such as Tampa General Hospital and Mary Hitchcock Memorial Hospital. Failing to “capitulate” would cost the plaintiff Massachusetts hospitals “tens of millions of dollars in annual losses” collectively.

However, the new complaint goes on to criticize Lilly’s 340B ESP platform, “a unilaterally selected, private-equity-owned third-party platform” whose use is required by Lilly as “a data dragnet that, so far, has been free from public scrutiny.”

The hospitals said the platform’s claim to de-identify submitted claims data is superficial, and through non-negotiable contractual terms allows the company to use data beyond integrity review while limiting the company’s liability to “the unconscionable sum of $100.” Hospitals forced to use the platform to receive the drug discounts, meanwhile, take on “the full burden of privacy, cybersecurity and regulatory compliance risk” related to the claims data, which they said is protected health information under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

“Lilly has thus intentionally abused its substantial market power to create an untenable ‘Sophie’s choice’: Plaintiffs must capitulate to Lilly’s demands, incur higher operational costs, and surrender valuable privacy-protected Claims Data on non-negotiable Terms, or forgo their 340B savings, absorb substantially higher upfront drug costs, and jeopardize services for vulnerable patients,” they wrote in the complaint.

The hospitals wrote that the practices violate Massachusetts’ consumer protection law and are unjustly enriching the company. They asked the court to enjoin Lilly from enforcing its policy and terms, and to require compensatory damages “including but not limited to the amounts plaintiffs have paid to defendant above the applicable 340B Ceiling Price, lost 340B savings and other consequential damages.”

Fierce Healthcare has reached out to Lilly for comment on the attorney general’s letter and the new lawsuit. Lilly has historically disputed hospitals’ portrayal of its policy’s burdens in prior litigation and asserted that the required data is already routinely collected by the organizations.

This week’s developments add to the substantial legal showdowns surrounding the controversial drug discount program, which has ballooned in scale over the last several years. Drugmakers critical of the program have sought to impose restrictions on contract pharmacies, prompting litigation in states where lawmakers have prohibited such restrictions.

Policymakers’ own appetite to curb the subsidy program, such as through a pilot rebate program, has also spurred its fair share of litigation.

Even so, no one predicted the post would catch fire the way it did. Eagle-eyed readers will have spotted that she has the same surname as me – that’s because she’s my mom. She’s one of two people closer to the Healthline home who spoke to me for this piece and whose stories echo the Facebook post thread, with the good and the hard bits side by side.

Kitty was a hockey player and distance runner as a girl, the kind who could eat what she liked without it showing. Then came children, and a weight that crept up year after year, with decades of diets that “worked for a couple of months only to be back to my former weight and then some.” In late 2024, after a lot of research, she decided to try tirzepatide.

Since then, Kitty has lost 56 pounds. She says her joints hurt less, which she puts down to reduced inflammation. She is more mobile. The constant chatter about food has faded. She thinks harder before she eats now.

“I am a strong believer that obesity is a disease and in my case certainly not the result of overindulgence or laziness,” she says. “GLP-1s have greatly improved my health and self-esteem. After years of struggling with my weight, I have finally found a way to solve the problem.”

Kitty will also tell you what it cost. Nausea. Constipation. Sulfur-tasting burps and rough nights of indigestion. Initial hair loss that has since grown back, and loose skin. Muscle loss. And she misses some of the true joy of cooking and eating food.

But despite bumps in her journey, Kitty remains convinced that the benefits of GLP-1s for her life and health outweigh any drawbacks she’s experienced. “I am conscious that I will probably be on this drug for a long time if not for life. It is truly a miracle drug.”

The cost conversation should account for the potential benefits of preventing or reducing those complications.”

The treatment may be what’s keeping the condition controlled. Obesity, she argues, deserves the same approach. “Reaching a goal weight can mean treatment is working, and decisions about continuing it should reflect the patient’s ongoing needs, benefits and side effects.”

It’s important to note that this article looks at the experiences of only a handful of people out of the millions who now take GLP-1s. Like all drugs, these medications are not right for everyone. Side effects are common; some of them can be serious, and both the long-term impact and what happens when people stop are still being worked out. Ultimately, your care team, with knowledge of your medical history, is a far better guide than a comment thread.

Yet the evidence now reaches well beyond the scales: large trials have linked semaglutide to fewer heart attacks and strokes in people with heart disease and slower kidney decline in people with diabetes and kidney disease, and tirzepatide became the first FDA-approved drug for moderate to severe sleep apnea in adults with obesity.

Stories like Kitty’s, Ann Marie’s, Mayim Bialik’s, and those of the 3.5k commenters on the Facebook post aren’t proof of what will happen to you one way or another. But for a lot of people, my mom included, these drugs have changed what an ordinary day looks like, and in many cases changed what their future can look like.

One of the most significant drug developments of the century so far? Hard to argue with.

#19. ACOG Strongly Recommends Hormone Therapy for Menopause Symptoms

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) published updated recommendations for menopause management in Obstetrics & Gynecology on October 15.

These expanded recommendations are the most comprehensive guidance to date. They include new clinical guidelines for menopause hormone therapy (MHT), nonhormonal medications, and nonpharmacological treatment of vasomotor symptoms (VMS), which include hot flashes and night sweats, and sleep and mood changes.

According to the ACOG guidance, 50%–92% of females experiencing menopause report VMS.

Around 16%–66% of females in the United States report menopause-related sleep issues, and more than one-third of perimenopausal and postmenopausal females report experiencing depressive symptoms.

“In recent years, misconceptions about the risks of hormone therapy have been widespread, which has made some clinicians reluctant to prescribe it and some patients hesitant to use it,” Bruce Pier, MD, clinical professor and associate fellowship director at the University of North Carolina and collaborator on the ACOG clinical practice guidelines, told Healthline.

“This guidance offers a comprehensive review of the evidence to support individualized counseling and shared decision making. Our hope is that it clears up those misconceptions and expands access to treatment.”

The ACOG is also developing companion guidance for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, expected in 2027.

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of MHT with estrogen and progestogens (or estrogen alone for people without a uterus) for both VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbance, in individuals who can use hormones.

It also conditionally recommends conjugated estrogen plus bazedoxifene (Duavee) as an alternative for these issues.

According to a meta-analysis from 2021 that was cited by the guidelines, estrogen therapy, with or without progestogens, significantly reduced VMS frequency and severity compared to a placebo or no treatment. A 2017 meta-analysis ranked transdermal estradiol plus a progestogen as the most effective option for reducing VMS frequency.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis, also cited by the guidelines, found that MHT was associated with improved self-reported sleep quality. Once again, transdermal therapies showed some of the largest effects.

The ACOG recommends initiating MHT before age 60 or within 10 years of the onset of natural menopause..

Research shows that factors such as race, culture, socioeconomic status, gender identity, and sexual orientation can influence how individuals experience menopause and the care they receive.

According to the SWAN study, Black women reported more menopause symptoms than white women but were less likely to be treated for them.

“This updated document shines a light on these inequities. Clinicians can proactively help right now by asking every patient in midlife about symptoms, rather than waiting for them to bring it up. Long term, we hope this document drives more research funding to understand these gaps and begin closing them,” said Pier.

The ACOG stated in the guidelines that, “The available evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against the use of estrogen–progestogen or estrogen-only MHT for the treatment of menopause-related depressive symptoms.”

It recommends that perimenopausal and postmenopausal individuals who meet the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD) or those whose most noticeable symptom is depression should be treated according to depression management guidelines.

Current treatment guidelines for depression include therapy, medication, or a combination of the two.

Psychological treatments for depression include psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), while antidepressants are among the most commonly prescribed medications.

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) for the management of menopausal symptoms like VMS and depression in individuals who don’t want to use MHT or can’t use it.

Currently, paroxetine is the only antidepressant that is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the management of VMS.

The ACOG also strongly recommends the use of neurokinin receptor antagonists to manage VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbances in people who don’t desire hormone therapy or can’t use it.

There are currently two neurokinin receptor antagonists approved by the FDA to manage menopause symptoms. Fezolinetant (Veozah) was approved in 2023 to help manage hot flashes. In 2025, the FDA approved elinzanetant (LYNKUET) for the management of moderate to severe VMS due to menopause.

#20. A Handful of Pistachios May Help You Sleep Better, Review Says

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Could a handful of pistachios before you go to bed really help you sleep better?

A review of studies published in Nutrition Research Reviews examines how the nutrients and plant compounds in pistachios might influence sleep health.

Pistachios contain several nutrients linked to sleep-related processes, prompting an investigation into whether they might offer benefits beyond their nutritional value.

While the findings suggest that pistachios may support sleep, the evidence is inconclusive and warrants further study.

“Pistachios are compelling for sleep research because they bring together several nutrients and plant compounds connected to sleep physiology within one whole food,” said study author Marie-Pierre St-Onge, PhD, professor of nutritional medicine and director of the Center of Excellence for Sleep & Circadian Research in the Department of Medicine at Columbia University Institute of Nutrition, in a news release.

“Rather than focusing on a single ‘sleep nutrient,’ this review provides a rationale for studying how the complete pistachio food matrix may affect sleep-related pathways,” she continued.

The narrative review examined existing research on pistachios and sleep-related biology.

The researchers explored whether the combination of nutrients in pistachios could promote quality sleep.

Rather than identifying pistachios as a sleep remedy, the researchers outlined biological pathways through which their nutrients and bioactive compounds might influence sleep.

“We were particularly interested in pistachios because they are particularly high in phytomelatonin, polyphenols, and carotenoids, which we noted were plant-derived components that had been shown to have sleep benefits in preclinical studies,” St-Onge told Healthline.

“We also noted their high content of tryptophan and vitamin B6, which are relevant to melatonin production in humans,” she continued.

Pistachios contain several nutrients associated with sleep, including melatonin, which helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle.

Other studies examining the effects of some of these nutrients on sleep offer further context.

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of tryptophan supplementation found that it may reduce wakefulness after sleep onset, particularly at doses of at least 1 gram (g) daily. However, an ounce of pistachios supplies only about 71 mg, which is much less than the doses examined by the researchers.

Additionally, a 2023 review of dietary polyphenols led by St-Onge identified potential links between plant compounds and sleep outcomes, although results varied across studies.

Neither review established that polyphenols or tryptophan from pistachios specifically affected sleep quality.

“I was not surprised that pistachios may help support sleep, given their combination of nutrients involved in sleep regulation,” said Michelle Routhenstein, MS, a preventive cardiology dietitian at EntirelyNourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“Pistachios provide tryptophan, an amino acid the body uses to produce serotonin and melatonin; vitamin B6, which supports these pathways; and magnesium, which plays a role in nervous system function and sleep regulation,” she told Healthline.

The new review highlights the concept of the “food matrix,” or how nutrients and bioactive compounds interact within a whole food.

Instead of investigating tryptophan, magnesium, or melatonin individually, researchers were curious about the combined effects of consuming them together in pistachios.

Laboratory and simulated-digestion research offers clues about how fiber and plant compounds in pistachios and peanuts interact with gut microbes. However, these findings do not establish that eating pistachios changes sleep quality in real-world conditions.

Broader nutrition research also points to a relationship between diet and sleep.

A 2021 review in the Annual Review of Nutrition examined evidence linking dietary patterns and individual nutrients to sleep outcomes. The findings suggest a relationship between sleep and diet, but note that more research is needed to determine cause and effect.

In a nutshell, the current evidence builds a compelling case for pistachios as a sleep aid.

However, it’s too soon to recommend pistachios as a sleep remedy, as many factors may affect sleep quality and insomnia.

“Sleep difficulties can have different underlying causes,” Routhenstein noted. “If inadequate intake of these nutrients is a contributing factor, pistachios may help, but they may not address sleep issues related to other nutrient deficiencies or other causes.”

Much of the supporting evidence cited in the review comes from studies of individual nutrients, other foods, laboratory experiments, or biological mechanisms rather than from trials involving whole pistachios.

Researchers have also reported significant variations in the melatonin content of pistachios, making the amount in a typical serving difficult to pinpoint.

Additionally, the review received support from American Pistachio Growers, and the corresponding author, St-Onge, serves on its advisory board. Larger studies and clinical trials are needed, ideally without industry funding, to ensure well-rounded results.

Indeed, the authors call for randomized controlled trials examining whether eating pistachios affects sleep outcomes, including sleep quality, duration, and melatonin levels.

“This was a review; we noted that the complete profile of pistachios would make them well-suited to support healthful sleep, but research is needed to evaluate this,” St-Onge told Healthline.

“This is the next step, and something we are actively working on. We also need to better understand what dosage and frequency of consumption would be optimal.”

Regardless, a handful of pistachios is still considered a healthy choice as part of a balanced diet.

“A small handful, about 1 ounce of shelled pistachios, is an appropriate portion,” Routhenstein said. “Enjoy them during the day or as a light afternoon or evening snack when you’re hungry.”

If nutrients like tryptophan help promote sleep, it’s possible that other nuts could yield similar results.

“Other nuts, such as almonds and walnuts, may offer similar benefits because they also contain nutrients involved in sleep regulation,” Routhenstein said.

“Early research is encouraging, but it’s too soon to say how much they might help.”

Other foods, like turkey, also contain tryptophan.

“For someone allergic to nuts, foods such as tofu, beans, oats, eggs, dairy, and fish can provide tryptophan, which the body uses to make serotonin and melatonin,” Routhenstein said. “The goal is to meet their nutrient needs rather than rely on one food to improve sleep.”

Sleep health depends on multiple factors. As such, Routhenstein recommends a personalized approach to getting better sleep, from what you eat and when you eat to addressing any nutritional gaps.

“I would also encourage regular, balanced meals throughout the day to support blood sugar management, especially for those with diabetes, while avoiding large meals close to bedtime,” Routhenstein said. “Caffeine later in the day and alcohol in the evening are also worth considering as possible sleep disruptors.”

#33. Michael Strahan Shares the Words That Helped His Daughter Through Brain Cancer Treatment

📅 Oct 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

For years, Michael Strahan appeared in commercials for St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, delivering a message he never imagined would become so personal: “No parent should have to hear that their child has cancer.”

But in 2023, the beloved TV host and former NFL player faced that reality when his daughter, Isabella, was diagnosed with brain cancer at 19 years old.

“[That] line that I used to deliver, usually at the end of the commercial…here I am actually filling that line in my life in real time,” Strahan told Healthline.

Strahan believes his longtime connection with St. Jude was more than a coincidence. After years of advocating for families affected by childhood cancer, he found himself on the other side of that experience.

“When I needed that family…they were there for me,” he said. “I’m just appreciative, and I think things come back to you; give out good, good comes back.”

In October 2023, Isabella began experiencing vomiting, headaches, and balance issues during her freshman year at the University of Southern California (USC). Tests revealed that she had medulloblastoma, a fast-growing cancerous brain tumor located in the cerebellum. She underwent emergency brain surgery, radiation therapy, and four rounds of chemotherapy.

While medulloblastoma typically occurs in children between the ages of 3 and 9, it can also affect young adults like Isabella.

“[The] biology in adolescents and young adults can differ meaningfully from pediatric cases, requiring specialized expertise at centers of excellence for this disease,” Arnab Chakravarti, MD, director of the Brain Tumor Program at The Ohio State University, told Healthline.

Medulloblastoma is sometimes confused with glioblastoma, the most common type of malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Glioblastoma is particularly aggressive, with a median overall survival of just 15 months and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.

However, Chakravarti explained that medulloblastoma has a significantly higher cure rate than glioblastoma.

“[This is] a point of genuine hope that deserves to be communicated clearly,” he said.

#42. Oral GLP-1 Orforglipron (Foundayo) Achieves Weight Loss for Women of All Ages

The findings were presented at the Annual Meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD), held in Milan, Italy, from September 28 to October 2.

This is important because menopause often causes weight gain due to lower estrogen levels, making weight loss more difficult for many women.

In April of this year, Eli Lilly’s Foundayo received FDA approval as a weight-loss drug, making it the second GLP-1 pill for weight loss to enter the U.S. market after Novo Nordisk’s Wegovy.

The ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 studies involved women who were either obese or overweight and who had at least one medical condition related to their weight.

Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher, and overweight as a BMI of 27 or higher.

The participants were divided into groups based on their menopausal status: premenopausal (before menopause), perimenopausal (during the menopause transition), and postmenopausal (after menopause).

Each group included between 142 and 225 females who either received orforglipron or placebo, an inactive pill with none of the drug in it.

The ATTAIN-1 study focused on people who were overweight or obese but did not have diabetes. ATTAIN-2 included participants who had both overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes.

This reduction is important because greater visceral fat is associated with higher risks of heart disease and diabetes.

Losing 5% or more of body weight is often considered clinically meaningful, as it can lead to improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels.
