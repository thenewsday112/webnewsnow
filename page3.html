#23. Women With Autoimmune Diseases More Likely to Be Misdiagnosed Than Men

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new international study found that women with autoimmune diseases often experience longer delays in getting a correct diagnosis compared to men.

The research, published in eClinicalMedicine on October 12, also shows that females report higher rates of being misdiagnosed, especially with mental health or psychosomatic conditions.

These findings highlight challenges in diagnosing autoimmune diseases and suggest that sex differences affect patients’ experiences in healthcare.

This is significant given that autoimmune diseases, conditions in which the body’s immune system attacks its own healthy cells, are estimated to affect around 4.6% of the U.S. population. Additionally, despite the observed disparities in diagnoses, women are almost two times as likely as men to be diagnosed with an autoimmune disease.

Researchers conducted an online survey between December 7, 2024, and March 7, 2025, inviting adults ages 18 and older who had a confirmed autoimmune or rheumatic disease.

The survey was carefully designed and tested with input from patients, charities, and medical experts to ensure it captured important information about patients’ diagnostic journeys.

Participants were asked about the time it took from when they first noticed symptoms to when they received their autoimmune diagnosis.

They also reported how many healthcare visits they made before diagnosis and whether they felt their symptoms were ever misdiagnosed as mental health issues, psychosomatic problems (physical symptoms caused or worsened by psychological factors), or another physical disease.

The survey was widely shared on social media, patient support groups, and community organizations to reach a broad audience.

Special efforts were made to include underrepresented groups such as males, younger individuals, and people from minority ethnic backgrounds.

The survey was available in five languages, and participants could complete it with help from researchers if needed.

To analyze the data, the team used statistical methods, adjusting for factors like age, type of autoimmune disease, continent of residence, and financial situation.

These methods helped compare the experiences of males and females while accounting for differences in disease types and other factors

The study included 6,607 participants, with 91.2% identifying as female and most living in the United Kingdom. On average, women were younger than men in the study.

Autoimmune thyroid disease was the most common condition reported. Non-autoimmune conditions like anxiety were more commonly reported by women, while men reported higher rates of hypertension.

One of the main findings was that women reported longer delays before getting a diagnosis. Nearly 32% of females experienced delays of four years or more from symptom onset to diagnosis, compared to about 26% of males.

On the other hand, men were more likely to be diagnosed within a year of symptom onset (nearly 50% of males versus 40% of females).

Women also made more visits to healthcare providers before diagnosis, with 26.2% having 11 or more visits compared to 18.4% of men.

When it came to misdiagnoses, women were significantly more likely to report that their symptoms had been mistaken for mental health issues (43.3% of females vs. 32.7% of males) or psychosomatic causes (33.9% of females vs. 21.5% of males).

However, there was no significant difference between men and women in being misdiagnosed with another physical disease.

The type of autoimmune disease also influenced misdiagnosis rates. Diseases with clear physical symptoms or definitive lab tests, like type 1 diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis, had lower rates of mental health and psychosomatic misdiagnoses.

In contrast, conditions such as autoimmune encephalitis or undifferentiated connective tissue disease had higher rates of these misdiagnoses (over 50%).

After adjusting for disease type and other factors, women still had higher odds of reporting mental health or psychosomatic misdiagnoses compared to men.

Other factors affected misdiagnosis rates, too. People living in continents outside the United Kingdom, those with lower household income, and certain disease groups were more likely to report psychiatric or psychosomatic misdiagnoses. Age, however, was not linked to the risk of being misdiagnosed.

Muhammad Qasim, MD, a pulmonologist and medical writer with MySleepApneaTeam, told Healthline that autoimmune diseases are often difficult to diagnose. Qasim wasn’t involved in the study.

“Early symptoms can be vague, fluctuate over time, and affect several parts of the body,” he said. “Fatigue, pain, sleep problems, brain fog, and mood changes do not immediately point to one diagnosis.”

Qasim echoed the study’s findings, explaining that similar symptoms may be interpreted differently in women versus men, with complaints being attributed to anxiety, stress, or psychosomatic causes.

However, there are probably multiple factors contributing to this phenomenon, he said.

“Differences in disease presentation, gaps in research, fragmented care, and unconscious bias can all contribute,” said Qasim. “Once a psychiatric explanation enters the medical record, there is also a risk that later clinicians view new symptoms through that same lens.”

Regarding unconscious bias, the study’s authors noted that women have historically been treated by doctors as “less credible” and more “emotional” in reporting their symptoms, which can influence how doctors interpret them.

These disparities in diagnosis are important, however, because longer waits and erroneous diagnoses can delay proper treatment and increase women’s frustration, leading them to stop asking for help.

The researchers suggest that healthcare providers need to be aware of these concerns and consider autoimmune diseases carefully when patients, especially females, present with more complicated symptoms.

Adam Carewe, MD, a physician in Boulder, CO, and a clinical informaticist with General Medicine who wasn’t involved in the study, offered guidance to those experiencing symptoms on how to obtain a proper diagnosis.

Breast cancer awareness has never been higher. Every October, the world turns pink and the key messages—that early diagnosis saves lives and that knowing the risk factors reduces the risk—are hard to miss.

Breast cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death amongst women in most countries, with an estimated 694,000 deaths annually and around 8.2 million women globally living with a breast cancer diagnosis, according to 2024 data from the World Health Organization (WHO)’s Global Health Observatory.

But when October ends, and the wall-to-wall campaigns move on, what has awareness actually changed for those impacted by breast cancer?

For Marisa Weiss, MD, the answer is that there is always more to be done, and she’s uniquely placed to hold that view.

Dr. Weiss has treated breast cancer patients for more than 30 years. In 2000, she founded Breastcancer.org, a not-for-profit that provides expert-reviewed medical and lifestyle information on breast health and breast cancer to millions of people globally.

Sixteen years ago, she was diagnosed with stage 1 breast cancer, becoming, in her own words, a “dual citizen” in that moment, with lived experience on both sides of the doctor’s desk.

I had the pleasure of sitting down with Dr. Weiss as part of Healthline’s Breast Cancer Awareness Month coverage to talk about how awareness has changed care for the better for millions of people around the world, but why critical gaps remain, and how access to trusted information and community can help close those gaps.

“It’s good that awareness is high, but knowledge remains low,” she said. The misconception she sees most is the belief that breast cancer is something that happens to other families.

“Most people think they’re off the hook because it doesn’t run in their family,” Dr. Weiss explained. In fact, 85%-90% of people who get breast cancer have no known family history and no known gene in the family, “so we’re all at risk,” she warns.

For most, a diagnosis arrives as a shock. “They’re completely surprised, flattened by it. Their life is upside down, and they scramble to find what they need to know,” Dr. Weiss noted.

That aftershock caused by a diagnosis is at the core of why Breastcancer.org exists. Because the person whose life is at stake is increasingly expected to direct their own care and advocate for their needs, access to accurate and empowering information is critical in that process. “Decisions aren’t being made around the patient and for the patient in a bubble,” Dr. Weiss said. “Decision-making is supposed to be shared.”

With appointments hard to get and clinicians stretched, she said patients have to do more of the work.

In practice, Dr. Weiss says that means preparing by writing down your family history on both sides and making sure your care team knows who you are. “What’s important to you? Who’s in your life? Do you want more children? Are you planning a vacation? All those decisions can have an impact on the care you receive.”

Where Dr. Weiss sees the widest gap is after treatment ends. Speaking from personal experience, she said: “I had the best care possible, and there were still gaps. You can find yourself alone with lots of questions and fears.”

Understandably, a great deal of focus and attention is centered on diagnosis and treatment, she said. Then it stops. “When your life is supposed to go back to normal, it never does. You have to find a new normal. That quiet phase can be really lonely and difficult.”

For Dr. Weiss, the questions that fill that silence are often as practical as they are emotional: “How do I know my treatment is working? How do I tell leftover side effects from new ones? How do I go back to work, and who do I tell? How do I talk to my kids? What about that new treatment I heard about on TV last night?”

That post-treatment period can also be the first time anyone has really had a chance to stop, take stock, and start asking “what next?”

“You might think, what happened to me? Who do I want to be moving forward? How do I have a long life full of wonderful things, and remain as independent as possible?”

Some of what patients do post-care never reaches their doctor at all, with Dr. Weiss pointing to a growing amount of personal care happening outside the hospital: GLP-1s, peptides, hormonal therapies, supplements, medical cannabis, to name a few. Breastcancer.org’s annual Pulse survey shows that people reported being interested in all of those things and more, and that they tend to learn about them online rather than risk the stigma of bringing them up with their doctors.

“They don’t really trust that their doctor knows much about these things,” she said, “and they don’t want a conversation that makes them feel judged. Some patients worry about what ends up in their medical record and then everyone’s going to know.”

Dr. Weiss says the stakes are real, because some of those choices can interact with cancer medications with potential for adverse or unexpected side effects. “That is why people turn to Breastcancer.org with questions that are personal, private, and important, including “what do I need to share with my doctor, and who’s on a need-to-know basis?”

The same community can also be intrusive. Dr. Weiss describes standing at a party, drink in hand, when someone asks what kind of cancer you had, what you did, and whether it came back. Her advice to patients, which she took herself: “Put up your hand and say, ‘thank you so much for caring, but I cannot hear another story right now.’”

On the topic of AI and its impact on treatment and care, Dr. Weiss is cautiously optimistic. On the treatment front, she points to tools that help radiologists read mammograms more thoroughly and assess future risk as game changers in the fight against breast cancer.

Breastcancer.org also uses AI for tasks such as finding citations and interpreting pathology reports. But like many, she is wary of misinformation. “When you’re fearful, you look for reassurance, and you don’t want to be reassured by misinformation. When it comes to health information, every word matters.”

Her final advice is for the appointment itself, which she says she has even struggled with. “When they start speaking quickly in complex medical language, you can get lost really fast. The risk is you kind of fake it. That must not happen.”

“Do your research in advance. Write things down. Bring someone you trust as a second set of ears. Ask direct questions. Don’t worry if it sounds funny or silly. It’s always a smart question when you are dealing with your health.” Just be prepared for the questions to multiply from there. “Once you get a certain set of questions answered, that raises more questions. So just write it all down.”

#25. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Not Be Linked to Colorectal Cancer

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Ultra-processed foods as a group do not increase the risk of colorectal cancer, according to a new study.

Researchers from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) report that they found no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer incidence among the more than 400,000 adults they studied.

They said their findings are important because ultra-processed foods now account for more than half of adults’ energy intake in the United States. They published their research on October 15 in PLOS Medicine.

The study comes amid recent research that has connected ultra-processed foods to an increased risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.

However, the link between colorectal cancer and ultra-processed foods has been less clear.

A 2025 study reported that ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of colorectal cancer in females under 50.

A 2022 study found that males who consumed high rates of ultra-processed foods had a higher risk of developing colorectal cancer.

NCI scientists noted in their new study that ultra-processed products represent a wide range of foods.

They pointed out that foods such as whole grains and dairy products were associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer among study participants, while foods such as processed meats were associated with a higher risk.

“We did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence,” said Erikka Loftfield, PhD, a former investigator with the NCI, in a news release. “However, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention.”

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the NCI scientists appear to be on the right track.

“While the study did not find that overall ultra-processed food intake was associated with colorectal cancer risk, the findings suggest that simply classifying foods by their level of processing may not tell the whole story,” said Marissa Shams-White, PhD, a senior principal scientist in population science at the American Cancer Society.

“One of the challenges with studying ultra-processed foods is that the category includes a very wide range of foods and beverages, and we don’t yet know whether they all relate to colorectal cancer risk in the same way,” she added.

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, agrees.

“Just because it is ultra-processed doesn’t mean it is inherently bad or good for you,” he said. “The source of the food and the additives like preservatives, flavorings, sugars, and pigments that are added to increase palatability probably play a much larger role in increasing cancer risk.”

“A whole grain cereal and a hot dog are both UPF, but they push risk in opposite directions,” Thanki noted.

In their study, the National Cancer Institute researchers analyzed data from 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71 years who enrolled in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study between 1995 and 1996.

The participants recorded their eating habits using a food frequency questionnaire. Researchers then used that data to estimate each person’s intake of ultra-processed foods. The median consumption of ultra-processed foods among participants was 43% of daily median intake.

The participants were then followed until 2018, for a median of nearly 19 years. The scientists reported that 10,075 cases of colorectal adenocarcinoma were diagnosed during that period.

The researchers reported no association between overall intake of ultra-processed foods and the incidence of colorectal cancer.

However, they said their findings varied by food type. Higher intake of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium derived from UPF sources was associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence, while meat from UPF sources was associated with higher incidence.

Researchers also found that replacing 10% of gram weight from UPF intake with minimally processed foods was associated with a 2% lower incidence of CRC.

The researchers acknowledged limitations to their study.

They noted that the participants were primarily older, white Americans. In addition, UPF intake was only measured once at the beginning of the study period, so the findings cannot account for changes in eating habits or the increasing availability of ultra-processed foods.

Nonetheless, Ashkan Farhadi, MD, a gastroenterologist at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in California, who wasn’t involved in the study, said he found the results “very interesting.”

“The definition of ultra-processed foods is so broad,” he told Healthline. “It’s important to designate what type of ultra-processed foods you’re talking about.”

“You shouldn’t just go by whether a food is designated as ultra-processed,” Farhadi added. “You should go by what’s on the [ingredient] label.”

Shams-White shared a similar approach. “When it comes to diet, it is important to follow an overall healthy dietary pattern,” she told Healthline. “Emphasize a variety of fruits and vegetables and whole grains and limit red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra-processed foods, and refined grain products.”

“Eating a diet rich in fiber and whole grains, dairy and calcium, and limiting processed and red meat and alcohol is what really reduces your risk of developing cancer,” added Thanki.

The American Cancer Society estimates there will be about 108,000 new cases of colon cancer and nearly 50,000 new cases of rectal cancer in the United States in 2026.

The incidence rate is slightly higher in men than in females.

The organization reports that the rate of colorectal cancer cases in the United States has declined since the mid-1980s, but they note that most of that decrease has occurred in older adults.

The incidence rate among people under 50 increased by nearly 3% from 2013 to 2022. Colorectal cancer is now the leading cause of death from cancer for U.S. adults under the age of 50.

Colorectal cancer occurs when cells in the large intestine grow out of control. About 70% of cases begin in the colon.

Diet is considered one of the risk factors for colorectal cancer. However, there are other considerations.

Farhadi said genetics, obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and a sedentary lifestyle can also increase a person’s risk of developing colorectal cancer.

Shams-White noted that the American Cancer Society Guideline for Diet and Physical Activity for Cancer Prevention recommended achieving and maintaining a healthy weight, being physically active, following a healthy eating pattern, and avoiding alcohol and tobacco.

Thanki points out that there are risk factors for colorectal cancer that can be modified and others that can’t.

“Among non-modifiable factors, the biggest are age, a personal or family history of colorectal cancer or advanced polyps, inherited syndromes such as Lynch syndrome, and longstanding inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s colitis),” he told Healthline.

“On the modifiable side, the evidence is strongest for obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, alcohol, and diets high in red and processed meat and low in fiber, whole grains, and calcium,” he added.

Colorectal cancer treatment outcomes improve if the disease is caught at an early stage. The 5-year relative survival for localized colorectal cancer is 91.3%. The 5-year relative survival rate for colorectal cancer caught at various stages, including cancer that has spread, is 65.4%.

Shams-White points out that the American Cancer Society Colorectal Cancer Guidelines recommend that people at average risk of colorectal cancer start getting screened at age 45.

“Screening can help prevent cancer by finding abnormal cells and polyps so they can be removed before they can become colorectal cancer, as well as detect cancer at an earlier stage,” she said.

Thanki had similar advice. “Get screened starting at 45, or earlier if you have a family history or other risk factors, because colonoscopies can prevent cancer by removing polyps,” he said. “And never ignore symptoms like rectal bleeding or a change in bowel habits at any age.”

Pope Leo XIV has been diagnosed with lung cancer following a successful surgery, the Vatican said.

The surgeons removed the lower lobe of the Pope’s lung to “ensure a radical procedure; the lower lobe of the right lung was removed using a video-assisted technique,” said Dr. Luigi Carbone, the Director of Vatican Healthcare Services, and Professor Stephano Margaritora, the lead surgeon, in a press release.

#27. RJ Mitte's Death Highlights Mental Health Challenges for People With Cerebral Palsy

“Breaking Bad” actor and disability advocate RJ Mitte has died at age 34.

Mitte reportedly died by suicide, his representative told People on October 12. However, local authorities have said the official cause and manner of his death remain under investigation.

Mitte, who was best-known for playing Walter White Jr. on the Emmy Award-winning television series “Breaking Bad,” lived with cerebral palsy and advocated for disability inclusion.

There is currently no evidence to suggest that cerebral palsy caused the actor’s death. Still, Mitte’s untimely passing highlights the mental health challenges that people with cerebral palsy may face.

Research has demonstrated elevated risks of anxiety and depression among those with the condition, alongside barriers to diagnosis, social participation, and employment.

“Living with cerebral palsy can affect mental health through several interacting pathways,” said Shannel Elhelou, neuropsychologist and founder of Brain Centric Neuropsychology in Westlake Village, CA.

“Chronic pain, fatigue, disrupted sleep, and the effort required to navigate daily activities can take an emotional toll. Experiences of bullying, discrimination, social exclusion, and difficulty accessing care can add to that burden. Also, the early brain injury underlying cerebral palsy may contribute to difficulties with emotional regulation, although each person’s experience is different,” Elhelou told Healthline.

The legacy he leaves behind underscores the need to recognize mental health as an essential part of care for individuals with cerebral palsy and other disabilities.

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a group of conditions affecting movement, posture, and coordination.

Most people with cerebral palsy have typical intelligence, although some also have intellectual disabilities or other comorbid physical and mental health conditions.

A 2018 study published in JAMA Neurology found that adults with cerebral palsy had a 28% higher risk of developing depression and a 40% higher risk of anxiety than adults without the condition.

A 2024 study in JAMA Network Open found higher recorded rates of anxiety and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) among children and young adults with cerebral palsy.

One concern is ‘diagnostic overshadowing,’ when symptoms of depression, anxiety, or other mental health conditions are attributed to cerebral palsy itself, leading those conditions to go unrecognized and untreated. Someone’s fatigue, withdrawal, or changes in behavior deserve careful assessment rather than the assumption that ‘this is just their cerebral palsy,'” she continued.

“While these findings help us understand mental health risks among people with cerebral palsy, they cannot tell us what any one person was experiencing. Many interacting factors may contribute to someone dying by suicide, and it would be inappropriate to attribute an individual’s death to their disability.”

#28. PMOS, Formerly PCOS, May Be Associated with Alzheimer's Disease Risk

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), formerly known as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), has been linked to changes in brain function and cognition that may overlap with those seen in Alzheimer’s disease.

However, a review published October 13 in the Journal of the Endocrine Society suggests that metabolic conditions commonly associated with PMOS, rather than PMOS itself, may help explain a potential link to Alzheimer’s disease and dementia risk.

“Researchers are exploring whether some metabolic conditions commonly seen in women with PMOS, such as insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes, [and] elevated cholesterol, could play a role in dementia, since these are already known risk factors for cognitive decline,” Yesika Garcia, MD, board certified endocrinologist with Medical Offices of Manhattan, who was not involved in the study, told Healthline.

“In other words, any potential connection may be related to these underlying metabolic issues rather than PMOS itself. I think it’s important to clarify that an association does not mean causation,” she added.

The researchers searched PubMed for studies published through 2025 examining brain structure and function, cognitive performance, biomarkers associated with neurodegenerative diseases, and dementia risk in people with PMOS.

Because PMOS is a newer term, the researchers searched for studies using both PMOS and PCOS.

Some brain imaging studies using MRI and PET scans identified differences in brain structure and function among people with PMOS. Researchers also observed hormonal changes associated with differences in gray matter.

Some studies identified elevated biomarkers associated with Alzheimer’s disease in people with PMOS. These findings appeared to overlap with metabolic changes seen in type 2 diabetes, particularly those associated with obesity and insulin resistance.

The researchers also found that neurological differences were less pronounced when people with PMOS were compared with metabolically healthy control groups, suggesting that associated metabolic conditions may play an important role in these findings.

Overall, the review suggests that metabolic conditions associated with PMOS may contribute to changes in brain health, although more research is needed to determine whether PMOS independently affects Alzheimer’s disease or dementia risk.

“I think we need to be careful about drawing conclusions too early. #37. Taking Blood Pressure Meds? DASH Diet Likely Safe for Potassium Levels

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

People who adhere to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet do not experience significant changes in potassium levels while taking blood pressure medications or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) drugs, according to a new study.

Researchers also reported that people taking these medications do not experience significant changes in their kidney function.

Both types of medications are known to increase potassium levels. High potassium levels can lead to hyperkalemia, a condition that can cause issues with the heart rhythm. People with impaired kidney function due to chronic kidney disease are at increased risk of hyperkalemia.

“The DASH diet is a well-known, proven nutrition pattern that is known to improve blood pressure,” said Marian Budu, MD, a clinical research coordinator on the GoFresh Trials at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston and the presenting author of the study, in a news release.

“Knowing that the DASH diet did not increase blood potassium levels in this population with normal baseline potassium levels provides assurance that the DASH diet can be prescribed to a wide range of people,” she continued.

The preliminary findings are being presented at the American Heart Association’s Hypertension Scientific Sessions 2026 meeting this week in Virginia. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

Experts not involved in the study told Healthline that the research provides medical professionals with important information.

“While we have traditionally exercised caution before recommending the DASH diet in patients who are prescribed ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or potassium-sparing diuretics, these results show that those patients could likely adopt the DASH diet without significant concern for harm,” said Cheng-Han Chen, MD, an interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA.

“DASH diets do not appear to increase potassium levels to any significant degree in patients taking medications that are known to potentially raise serum potassium,” added Kevin Volpp, MD, the founding director of the Penn Center for Health Incentives and Behavioral Economics in Pennsylvania and the scientific lead of the American Heart Association’s Health Care by Food initiative.

“This is important because it is a theoretical concern that diets rich in potassium could cause high levels in potassium in some patients.”

The new study was a secondary analysis of the GoFresh and GoFresh Rx randomized trials that enrolled Black adults in the greater Boston area with high blood pressure.

Some of the participants were taking medications to lower their blood pressure, and others were not taking any hypertension drugs.

The study focused on four classes of blood pressure medications: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), and potassium-sparing diuretics and beta-blockers, as well as NSAIDs.

The analysis took place in 2026 and included 356 Black adults with an average age of 53. About 70% were women.

At the start of the study, the participants had systolic blood pressure readings between 120 mm Hg and less than 150 mm Hg. They also had a diastolic blood pressure reading of less than 100 mm Hg.

The participants also had varying degrees of kidney function. In addition, they had limited access to grocery stores.

Half of the participants received three months of weekly DASH-patterned groceries and weekly nutrition counseling sessions. The other half received a cash stipend every four weeks to buy groceries, with no nutrition counseling.

The participants’ blood potassium levels were measured at the beginning of the study and again after 12 weeks.

The researchers reported that eating more potassium-rich foods in the DASH diet did not increase potassium levels to dangerous levels among participants taking blood pressure medications.

They noted no significant increase in potassium levels, regardless of whether participants were assigned to the dietary intervention or the monetary stipend group.

In a news release, the researchers acknowledged that their study did not include people with stage 4 or stage 5 kidney disease or those with higher-than-normal potassium levels.

They added that the study population comprised only Black individuals living in Boston, so the results may not generalize to the broader population.

The study authors said that future research should focus on participants with suboptimal kidney function and those with higher potassium levels.

Volpp agreed that more research is needed.

“This study was conducted in patients with non-elevated potassium levels at baseline and does not answer the question of whether they are safe in patients with elevated potassium levels or significant impairments in renal function,” he told Healthline.

Chen noted that potassium levels should be taken seriously.

“Certain classes of blood pressure medications can potentially cause high potassium levels, especially in people with kidney disease,” he told Healthline. “If potassium levels become too high, this can lead to symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and muscle pain, or even serious heart problems such as arrhythmias.”

The DASH diet is a dietary plan that emphasizes fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins such as chicken, fish, and beans.

The plan also includes whole grains, low fat dairy products, and cooking with vegetable oils. The diet discourages the intake of red meat, salt, fat, and added sugars.

Previous research has indicated that the DASH diet can provide numerous health benefits.

A preliminary 2022 study concluded that the dietary plan can help lower blood pressure and improve heart health, but those results haven’t been published in a scientific journal. Still, the findings were consistent with a 2019 study that found the DASH diet can help lower the risk of heart disease.

In addition, a 2020 study reported that adults aged 65 and older with obesity lost body fat while following the DASH Diet. A 2023 study suggested the diet can help reduce belly fat.

A 2019 meta-analysis suggested that the DASH diet may also help lower the risk of certain cancers.

Many of these protective effects are attributed to the diet’s high fruit and vegetable content.

Chen said the DASH diet has many benefits, but the eating plan might not be for everybody.

“The DASH diet has been shown to be beneficial in lowering blood pressure, losing weight, and controlling blood sugar and cholesterol levels,” he said. “However, the amount of planning and portion tracking required to follow the diet may be too much for some people.”

Chen recommended that people ease into the DASH diet.

“The best way to start on the DASH diet is to slowly introduce the changes to your meals,” he said. “This can be as simple as including a few extra servings of fruits and vegetables each day as well as swapping some whole grain products for refined grains. Over time, more of the diet can be incorporated.”

Volpp agreed that introducing the DASH diet in increments is a good strategy.

“For many people, it is easiest to make small incremental changes sequentially,” he said. “One way to get started is to increase fruit by a couple of servings a day. Another is to pay attention to the amount of sodium in packaged foods and consciously choose products lower in sodium. A third is to make a conscious effort to eat more vegetables.”

“Make changes first that are easiest for you to make; it will be easier to make those changes stick,” Volpp added.

The researchers measured several outcomes over a period of 72 weeks. They examined changes in body weight, waist circumference (the distance around the waist), and the waist-to-height ratio, a measure used to assess body fat distribution.

They also tracked how many women reached specific weight loss goals, such as losing at least 5%, 10%, 15%, or 20% of their body weight, compared to those who took the inactive pill.

By studying these factors, the researchers were able to determine not only whether orforglipron helped women lose weight but also how much weight loss was achieved and how it affected their body shape.

The results showed that women who took orforglipron experienced significant weight loss regardless of their menopausal status.

In the ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 studies, premenopausal, perimenopausal, and postmenopausal women all lost an average of up to 14% of their body weight.

This means that the medication was effective for women at different life stages, including those experiencing menopause, when weight gain is often a challenge.

In addition to losing body weight, women in the orforglipron groups also saw meaningful reductions in waist circumference. Waist measurements decreased by up to 12.5 centimeters (about 5 inches) across all menopausal groups in both studies.

This reduction is important because greater visceral fat is associated with higher risks of heart disease and diabetes.

The waist-to-height ratio also improved for many women taking orforglipron. More participants treated with the medication moved into lower waist-to-height ratio categories than those who took the placebo.

This indicates a healthier distribution of body fat and suggests a lower risk of heart disease.

When looking at the number of women who reached specific weight loss milestones, orforglipron performed much better than the placebo. Up to 83% of women on orforglipron lost at least 5% of their body weight, while 30% of women on placebo reached that same level of weight loss.

Losing 5% or more of body weight is often considered clinically meaningful, as it can lead to improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels.

Overall, these findings suggest that orforglipron is a promising new medication for weight management in women with obesity or overweight, including those who also have type 2 diabetes.

This can be especially helpful for those navigating the challenges of menopause while trying to manage their weight.

James J. Chao, MD, FACS, Chief Medical Officer and co-founder of VedaNu Wellness, wasn’t involved in the research, but said the significance of these findings for postmenopausal women.

“The 14% is what’s grabbing headlines,” he told Healthline, “but up to 12.5 cm from the waistline is what I’m interested in.”

According to Chao, as females enter postmenopause, they tend to store more fat around their midsection.

“This is the type of fat that can cause heart disease,” he said. “So a pill that can help a 38-year-old woman lose the same amount as a 62-year-old breaks that myth.”
