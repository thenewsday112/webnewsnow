#82. Hill-Climbing Agent Quality with Agents CLI Evaluation

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A live walkthrough of the Agents CLI eval loop, from a first run to regression tracking, model-backend experiments, and automated alerting

Building an agent that works on a demo is a different problem from building an agent that stays working across model upgrades, tool changes, and new conversation shapes. The first takes a weekend; the second takes a loop. A team writes test cases, runs them, grades them, reads what failed, changes the agent, and runs the cases again. If the score goes up, the change was good. If it goes down, revert. Over weeks of this, the agent inches toward the ceiling of what the model, tools, and prompt can do together. The quality-engineering name for this is hill-climbing.

The Agents CLI eval namespace was built around this loop. The commands are small and framework-agnostic: eval generate runs the agent against a dataset of cases, eval grade scores the generated traces, eval compare diffs two run results side by side, eval analyze clusters failures into themes, and eval optimize runs an automated hill-climbing pass on the agent's prompt.

The lesson is that the summary number is misleading. Look only at the mean and you'd call gemini-2.5-flash as bad as the chef prompt. Look at the per-case breakdown and you'd call it a reasonable swap for most of the agent's workload, with one case (handling out-of-scope questions about recent events) exposing a real limitation. If the production distribution of user questions matches the first three cases more than the fourth, the swap is worth considering. If off_topic-style questions are common, it is not.

Two caveats on this experiment. First, model swaps interact with the prompt: a prompt tuned for a stronger model often leaks capability when ported to a weaker one, so the right comparison is "weaker model with a prompt tuned for it" versus "stronger model with its own tuned prompt." Second, the Vertex AI Eval Service calls a judge model to grade the traces, and the judge is held constant across the comparison. Changing the judge is a different experiment and should be done separately.

Agents CLI does not ship a scheduler, but the eval commands are designed to drop into standard infrastructure. The pattern we can use here is a cron job or Cloud Scheduler trigger that runs eval generate plus eval grade on a cadence and writes the results to a persistent location:

A nightly run against a stable regression dataset catches drift from model updates, data changes in the agent's tools, and dependencies that shift under the agent's feet. For a production agent, the scheduled run also captures the model vendor's quiet upgrades, which can move the agent's behavior without any change on our side. The dataset for the scheduled run is typically larger than the one used in the inner loop (dozens to hundreds of cases rather than a few), since it only runs once a day.

The scheduled run produces a result file every day, but a file nobody looks at is not an alert. The pattern here can be a wrapper script that compares each new run's mean against a baseline (the previous run, or a committed known-good) and exits non-zero when the drop exceeds a threshold. The non-zero exit is what triggers the alert from the surrounding scheduler.

For ADK projects, eval optimize runs an automated hill-climbing pass on the agent's prompt using the GEPA framework (not currently supported for the LangChain extension). The command takes a dataset and a target metric, proposes prompt variants, grades each one, keeps the variants that improve the metric, and iterates. The output is a set of candidate prompts ranked by their eval score, which a human then reviews and promotes to the agent definition.

Two things to understand about eval optimize. First, it is scoped to prompt optimization, not to arbitrary agent changes: the agent's tools, structure, and model stay fixed. Second, GEPA sometimes finds genuine improvements and sometimes finds prompt variants that score higher on the specific metric by overfitting to its wording. The reviewer is the control. For projects where the loop applies, the discipline is to run eval optimize on the regression dataset (where improvements are measurable) and to review the candidate prompts carefully before promotion.

A few patterns and anti-patterns from running this loop ourselves and watching teams do the same.

Lead with a small, representative dataset, not a comprehensive one. The temptation when starting an eval is to try to cover every edge case the agent will ever see. The result is a dataset that takes an hour to run and that nobody runs on a change. Four cases that run in under a minute, as above, exercise the main behaviors and get run on every change. A regression dataset of three hundred cases that runs for an hour becomes a release gate at best and shelfware at worst.

Snapshot result files, not scores. The score is a summary; the result file is the evidence. When a regression surfaces three weeks after a prompt change, having the full result JSON from before and after the change (not just the mean score) is what lets eval compare show the specific case that moved. The per-case breakdown in Step 4 above would not have been possible from scores alone.

Separate the dataset from its expectations. The dataset is the inputs. The eval config is the expectations. They evolve on different cadences: the dataset grows as new real-world cases surface in production, and the config evolves as the team's definition of "good" matures. Keeping them in separate files, both committed to version control, makes it clear which half changed when a score moves.

Treat summary metrics with suspicion. The model-swap experiment in Step 5 is the clearest example. The mean dropped by half a point, but the per-case breakdown showed the drop was concentrated in one case while the others held or improved. A team that only looked at the mean would have made the wrong call.

More evals do not automatically lead to more fixes. A recent controlled study we ran found that coding agents using agents-cli run four times as many evals as coding agents without it, but change the agent after an eval about as often as the baseline. The behavior gain comes from what the skills put into the build at the start, not from the inner iteration. Evals are necessary for quality, but the quality comes from acting on what the evals show.

#13. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/eli-lillys-340b-data-policy-draws-new-opposition-missouri-ag-massachusetts-hospitals" hreflang="en">Eli Lilly's 340B data policy draws new opposition from Missouri AG, Massachusetts hospitals</a>

Eli Lilly's 340B data policy draws new opposition from Missouri AG, Massachusetts hospitals" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Eli Lilly’s policy requiring 340B hospitals submit claims-level data in order to receive statutory drug discounts garnered a fresh wave of pushback this week from Missouri’s attorney general and a cadre of Massachusetts hospitals, joining others who have described the requirements as unlawful.

Lilly announced its controversial policy for 340B dispensations in January and first put it into effect for a subset of hospitals in June. It applies to all pharmacy and medical dispensations, including in-house pharmacy and contract pharmacy dispenses, in most states.

The company said doing so would allow it to spot instances when a drug purchase is improperly granted multiple discounts under overlapping programs. Hospitals have contended that the extra scrutiny is unnecessary and introduces substantial administrative burden.

Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway has come in on the side of the opposition. On Monday, she issued a cease-and-desist letter to Lilly, writing that the policy violates the Missouri Merchandising Practices Act, a consumer protection law. If the drugmaker doesn’t end its requirement for Missouri providers, the policy “may result in subsequent legal action,” including an injunction and monetary civil penalties, the letter reads.

“Big Pharma does not get to bully Missouri hospitals or hold prescription drug prices hostage,” Hanaway said in an accompanying release. “Eli Lilly is abusing its market power to impose higher prescription prices in Missouri. These tactics fall hardest on rural communities that depend on the 340B program to keep clinics open and ensure patients receive the care they need.”

Hanaway’s letter and release highlight Lily’s decision to end discounts for University of Missouri Health Care in July. The system later agreed to provide the data as requested, but “now bears the heavy costs and burdens of complying with Lilly’s requirements,” the attorney general’s office wrote.

A day later, six Massachusetts hospitals hit Lilly with a new lawsuit to block the policy. Massachusetts General Hospital, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Salem Hospital, UMass Memorial Medical Center and Umass Memorial Health — Harrington Hospital filed the lawsuit.

Their complaint filed in federal court describes “corporate extortion” from “the largest pharmaceutical company in the world” that “deprives them of resources essential to serving their most vulnerable patients.”

The hospitals “rightfully refused to comply” with the policy and had their 340B discounts pulled, echoing the claimed injuries from others who have sued over the policy, such as Tampa General Hospital and Mary Hitchcock Memorial Hospital. Failing to “capitulate” would cost the plaintiff Massachusetts hospitals “tens of millions of dollars in annual losses” collectively.

However, the new complaint goes on to criticize Lilly’s 340B ESP platform, “a unilaterally selected, private-equity-owned third-party platform” whose use is required by Lilly as “a data dragnet that, so far, has been free from public scrutiny.”

The hospitals said the platform’s claim to de-identify submitted claims data is superficial, and through non-negotiable contractual terms allows the company to use data beyond integrity review while limiting the company’s liability to “the unconscionable sum of $100.” Hospitals forced to use the platform to receive the drug discounts, meanwhile, take on “the full burden of privacy, cybersecurity and regulatory compliance risk” related to the claims data, which they said is protected health information under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

“Lilly has thus intentionally abused its substantial market power to create an untenable ‘Sophie’s choice’: Plaintiffs must capitulate to Lilly’s demands, incur higher operational costs, and surrender valuable privacy-protected Claims Data on non-negotiable Terms, or forgo their 340B savings, absorb substantially higher upfront drug costs, and jeopardize services for vulnerable patients,” they wrote in the complaint.

The hospitals wrote that the practices violate Massachusetts’ consumer protection law and are unjustly enriching the company. They asked the court to enjoin Lilly from enforcing its policy and terms, and to require compensatory damages “including but not limited to the amounts plaintiffs have paid to defendant above the applicable 340B Ceiling Price, lost 340B savings and other consequential damages.”

Fierce Healthcare has reached out to Lilly for comment on the attorney general’s letter and the new lawsuit. Lilly has historically disputed hospitals’ portrayal of its policy’s burdens in prior litigation and asserted that the required data is already routinely collected by the organizations.

This week’s developments add to the substantial legal showdowns surrounding the controversial drug discount program, which has ballooned in scale over the last several years. Drugmakers critical of the program have sought to impose restrictions on contract pharmacies, prompting litigation in states where lawmakers have prohibited such restrictions.

Policymakers’ own appetite to curb the subsidy program, such as through a pilot rebate program, has also spurred its fair share of litigation.

#6. 6 to 8 Cups of Tea Daily May Reduce Heart Disease Risk by 11%

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

People who regularly drink tea may have an 11% lower overall risk of vascular disease, which includes conditions such as stroke, heart disease, and peripheral artery disease. This is according to a study published on October 21 in the Journal of Internal Medicine.

Both black tea and green tea may offer heart health benefits. However, the study shows an association rather than proving that drinking tea prevents cardiovascular disease.

The lowest overall vascular risk was seen among tea drinkers who were also physically active and didn’t smoke.

The study followed 485,934 people ages 30–79 from the China Kadoorie Biobank for an average of 9 years.

The researchers found that regular tea drinkers had an 11% lower overall risk of vascular disease compared with those who did not regularly drink tea. They also observed a 37% lower risk of ischemic stroke and a 17% lower risk of heart attack.

There was no clear link for hemorrhagic stroke or pulmonary heart disease.

Among people who drank tea at least weekly, the lowest estimated risk was seen in those consuming approximately 6–8 cups per day. However, the findings do not establish this as a recommended daily intake.

Amit Blumfield, MD, a cardiologist with Manhattan Cardiology in NYC who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that it is important to remember this is observational data.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining the effects of drinking black and green tea on lipids and blood pressure showed only modest, non-significant benefits.

The study also had limitations, including self-reported tea consumption and the possibility that other dietary and lifestyle factors influenced the results.

“Overall, tea seems to, maybe, have some cardiovascular benefits without a significant risk, so I favor tea consumption,” he said.

Michelle Routhenstein, a preventive cardiology dietitian at Entirely Nourished who was not involved in the research, emphasized that the findings do not establish a cause-and-effect relationship between drinking tea and a lower risk of vascular disease.

“Tea can be a helpful addition to a heart-healthy lifestyle, but it is not a substitute for addressing individual cardiovascular risk factors,” she said.

The researchers also found that drinking “burning hot” tea increased the risk of vascular disease by 16%, consistent with previous research raising concerns about the consumption of very hot beverages.

“I recommend enjoying your tea’s potential benefits by letting it cool to a comfortable sipping temperature before you drink it,” said Routhenstein.

The study examined black, green, Oolong, and other tea types.

The researchers found that black tea showed a more consistent association with a lower risk of vascular disease, which is contrary to some earlier research. However, they noted that these results don’t establish black tea as superior to green tea, and they don’t mean you should switch tea types.

“Both black and green tea are great choices for your heart health, and neither is definitively better than the other,” said Routhenstein.

“I recommend choosing whichever you enjoy and consuming it consistently, ideally without much added sugar,” Routhenstein noted.

Blumfield also noted that adding sugar or non-sugar sweeteners may change the potential health effects of drinking tea, so the findings should not automatically be extended to sweetened tea beverages.

#8. This Is How Fast You Should Walk to Help Lower Your Risk of Death

New research shows that how fast you walk, whether measured in a clinical test or through daily activity, can help predict your risk of dying from any cause.

The study compared two ways of measuring walking: a short walking speed test conducted in a clinic and the step rate people take in their everyday lives, measured with a wrist-worn device.

Both measures were linked to mortality risk and showed similar ability to predict it.

The findings, published on October 23 in JAMA Network Open, could help doctors and researchers choose the best method for assessing health risks in different settings.

The study was part of the Whitehall II accelerometer substudy in the United Kingdom.

It included 4,067 participants, mostly males, with an average age of about 69 years. About one-third of the subjects were female.

The researchers collected data from January 2012 to December 2013 and followed up through August 2024.

To measure clinical walking speed, participants completed a standard walking test. They walked a marked 2.44-meter course at their usual pace. The test was repeated three times, and the average speed in meters per second was used for analysis. Participants who normally used a cane were allowed to use it during the test.

For free-living walking measurements, participants wore a wrist-worn GENEActiv accelerometer for 9 consecutive days. This device recorded movement at a high frequency and used special software to detect walking episodes and count steps.

From this data, two key measures of walking cadence were calculated: the median walking cadence (the middle value of steps per minute during walking periods) and the 95th-percentile walking cadence (a higher step rate indicating brisk walking). The study also calculated the average number of daily steps.

The researchers additionally gathered information on factors known to affect mortality, such as:

National death records were used to identify deaths during the follow-up period.

Statistical methods were then used to estimate the risk of death associated with walking speed and cadence while accounting for other risk factors.

The models were adjusted step-by-step: starting with no adjustments, then adding sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, and finally health-related factors.

The ability of walking measures to predict mortality was assessed using a statistical measure.

The study also examined differences between males and females.

The researchers additionally conducted analyses to test how well the results held up when they excluded early deaths, looked at subgroups by age and health status, and used different definitions of walking cadence.

During a median follow-up of nearly 12 years, 739 participants died.

The average clinical walking speed of participants was 1.2 meters per second. The median walking cadence during daily life was about 75 steps per minute, and the 95th percentile walking cadence was about 112 steps per minute.

The study found only a weak correlation between clinical walking speed and free-living walking cadence, meaning the two measures do not strongly reflect the same walking behavior. However, both measures were independently associated with a lower risk of death.

After adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle, and health factors, for every standard increase in clinical walking speed, the risk of death was 23% lower.

Similarly, higher median and 95th-percentile walking cadences were associated with 17% and 18% lower risks of death, respectively.

When examining how well these walking measures predicted mortality, each on its own showed modest predictive ability.

Adding clinical walking speed to a model with other known risk factors slightly improved mortality prediction, while adding free-living walking cadence also improved prediction, albeit to a similar degree to that of clinical walking speed.

Combining clinical walking speed with free-living walking parameters provided a small boost in predictive ability, but the effect was not significant after accounting for detailed health factors.

The study also found that walking speed and cadence had similar predictive power for both men and women, although the overall prediction models performed slightly better for women.

Several sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. Results were consistent when excluding early deaths, focusing on healthier or older participants, and using different ways to define walking cadence.

Overall, this study suggests that clinical walking speed tests and free-living walking cadence measurements from wrist-worn devices are both useful in predicting all-cause mortality risk. They provide similar information but reflect different aspects of walking behavior.

The choice between methods may depend on the purpose of the assessment, available resources, and the context in which the measurement is taken.

Neha Sabharwal, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and director of rehabilitation specializing in geriatric mobility and functional decline at Vintage Faire Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Modesto, CA, said that walking speed helps predict longevity because it’s an indicator of someone’s overall health.

“Walking is something most of us do without thinking about it, but it requires several body systems to work together,” she told Healthline. “Your heart and lungs have to keep up, your muscles need to produce enough force, and your brain has to coordinate each step while keeping you balanced.”

Sabharwal, who wasn’t involved in the study, said that in her work, she pays attention to when someone starts walking more slowly than before.

“It is easy to dismiss that change as part of getting older, but there may be something else going on,” she said. This could include muscle weakness, pain, reduced endurance, balance problems, or underlying medical conditions.

According to Sabharwal, the current study is valuable because it suggests that useful information about people’s movement can be obtained outside a clinical setting.

However, she cautioned against misinterpreting the findings to mean that simply walking faster will make you live longer.

“People who naturally walk faster may already have better underlying health,” said Sabharwal.

“I would be less concerned about whether someone walks faster than their neighbor and more interested in whether they are walking slower than they did six months ago,” she said.

Theodore Strange, MD, chairman of medicine at Northwell Staten Island University Hospital and senior vice president of the Metro Market Region at Northwell Health, shared some insights for those who may notice their walking speed has declined. Strange wasn’t involved in the study.

“I mean, if it’s slowing down because a week or two ago, you were walking up a flight of steps with no problem, and now, you’re having some shortness of breath, I think it should be evaluated,” he told Healthline.

Strange added that if you’re experiencing aches or pains that weren’t there before, you should consult a healthcare professional, as they can make exercise more difficult.

It’s also essential to manage any chronic diseases that you have so that you can exercise as well as possible within the constraints of your condition, he said.

“And I think slow and steady wins the race here,” said Strange. He suggests monitoring your walking with a device like a pedometer and progressively increasing your steps.

“Everybody talks about 10,000 steps a day. I think the most recent studies show probably 7,000 to 8,000 is adequate,” he said. You can start where you are and work your way up gradually.

Strange further noted that physical therapy can sometimes be helpful if you have issues with gait mechanics, balance, or strength. Additionally, you might benefit from exercise that is tailored to your condition, for example, if you’ve had a stroke.

Finally, Strange advised stretching before and after exercise to keep your muscles moving.

“I go back to adequate nutrition and hydration being important,” he said. You want to make sure you are adequately supporting your body’s ability to exercise, said Strange.

GLP-1 medications have been shown to be effective for weight loss. However, the results may vary by sex and reproductive stage.

For example, studies have found that women and adults under age 55 may experience greater weight loss with these medications.

However, whether hormone therapy influences weight loss outcomes with different GLP-1 medications remains unclear.

To investigate this question, researchers compared weight loss in middle-aged women receiving either semaglutide or tirzepatide, with or without MHT. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

The study included 3,855 women with an average age of 57 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 36 kg/m2.

The semaglutide cohort included 2,379 individuals, while the tirzepatide cohort included 1,476 individuals.

The average follow-up in the semaglutide cohort, both with and without MHT, was 26 months. The average follow-up in the tirzepatide group was 17 months for those using MHT and 16 months for those not using it.

The researchers found that in the semaglutide cohort, those using MHT alongside the GLP-1 medication had significantly greater weight loss at 9 months than those not using MHT.

These differences were sustained at 12 and 15 months and at the final follow-up. Those using MHT were also more likely to reach up to a 30% total body weight loss than those not using it.

In the tirzepatide cohort, those using MHT had a modestly greater weight loss at 3 months and were more likely to achieve more than 5% total body weight loss by the final follow-up. However, no significant difference in weight loss was observed at later time points.

These findings suggest that MHT use may be associated with greater weight loss among middle-aged women taking semaglutide. However, the researchers did not observe a sustained, statistically significant difference in weight loss among those taking tirzepatide.

“This is an association, and the study provides an opportunity to discuss how menopause, aging, changes in body composition, and metabolic health can be present,” she said.

It is important to keep in mind that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved certain hormone therapy medications to treat menopause symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats, and vaginal dryness. However, these medications are not FDA-approved for weight loss or obesity management.

“I was not surprised that pistachios may help support sleep, given their combination of nutrients involved in sleep regulation,” said Michelle Routhenstein, MS, a preventive cardiology dietitian at EntirelyNourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“Pistachios provide tryptophan, an amino acid the body uses to produce serotonin and melatonin; vitamin B6, which supports these pathways; and magnesium, which plays a role in nervous system function and sleep regulation,” she told Healthline.

The new review highlights the concept of the “food matrix,” or how nutrients and bioactive compounds interact within a whole food.

Instead of investigating tryptophan, magnesium, or melatonin individually, researchers were curious about the combined effects of consuming them together in pistachios.

Laboratory and simulated-digestion research offers clues about how fiber and plant compounds in pistachios and peanuts interact with gut microbes. However, these findings do not establish that eating pistachios changes sleep quality in real-world conditions.

Broader nutrition research also points to a relationship between diet and sleep.

A 2021 review in the Annual Review of Nutrition examined evidence linking dietary patterns and individual nutrients to sleep outcomes. The findings suggest a relationship between sleep and diet, but note that more research is needed to determine cause and effect.

In a nutshell, the current evidence builds a compelling case for pistachios as a sleep aid.

However, it’s too soon to recommend pistachios as a sleep remedy, as many factors may affect sleep quality and insomnia.

“Sleep difficulties can have different underlying causes,” Routhenstein noted. “If inadequate intake of these nutrients is a contributing factor, pistachios may help, but they may not address sleep issues related to other nutrient deficiencies or other causes.”

Much of the supporting evidence cited in the review comes from studies of individual nutrients, other foods, laboratory experiments, or biological mechanisms rather than from trials involving whole pistachios.

Researchers have also reported significant variations in the melatonin content of pistachios, making the amount in a typical serving difficult to pinpoint.

Additionally, the review received support from American Pistachio Growers, and the corresponding author, St-Onge, serves on its advisory board. Larger studies and clinical trials are needed, ideally without industry funding, to ensure well-rounded results.

Indeed, the authors call for randomized controlled trials examining whether eating pistachios affects sleep outcomes, including sleep quality, duration, and melatonin levels.

“This was a review; we noted that the complete profile of pistachios would make them well-suited to support healthful sleep, but research is needed to evaluate this,” St-Onge told Healthline.

“This is the next step, and something we are actively working on. We also need to better understand what dosage and frequency of consumption would be optimal.”

Regardless, a handful of pistachios is still considered a healthy choice as part of a balanced diet.

“A small handful, about 1 ounce of shelled pistachios, is an appropriate portion,” Routhenstein said. “Enjoy them during the day or as a light afternoon or evening snack when you’re hungry.”

If nutrients like tryptophan help promote sleep, it’s possible that other nuts could yield similar results.

“Other nuts, such as almonds and walnuts, may offer similar benefits because they also contain nutrients involved in sleep regulation,” Routhenstein said.

“Early research is encouraging, but it’s too soon to say how much they might help.”

Other foods, like turkey, also contain tryptophan.

“For someone allergic to nuts, foods such as tofu, beans, oats, eggs, dairy, and fish can provide tryptophan, which the body uses to make serotonin and melatonin,” Routhenstein said. “The goal is to meet their nutrient needs rather than rely on one food to improve sleep.”

Sleep health depends on multiple factors. As such, Routhenstein recommends a personalized approach to getting better sleep, from what you eat and when you eat to addressing any nutritional gaps.

“I would also encourage regular, balanced meals throughout the day to support blood sugar management, especially for those with diabetes, while avoiding large meals close to bedtime,” Routhenstein said. “Caffeine later in the day and alcohol in the evening are also worth considering as possible sleep disruptors.”

#21. Ultra-Processed Food Intake May Increase Dementia, Stroke Risk

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Your hub for heart health management, insights, and solutions

Healthy adults who eat high levels of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) may be more likely to show signs of damage to the brain that could increase the likelihood of stroke and future memory problems, according to a study published October 15 in JAMA Network Open.

UPFs are often defined using the NOVA classification: These foods typically have 5 or more ingredients and include substances not typically found in culinary preparations. This category of foods includes packaged snack foods, sugary cereals, soft drinks, and frozen ready-to-eat meals.

UPFs account for more than 50% of daily energy intake in high-income countries, including the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom.

Previous research has linked these foods to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. This new study adds to growing evidence linking high UPF intake to adverse health outcomes.

This was a cross-sectional analysis of 8,783 adults between the ages of 35 and 69 without cardiovascular disease. The participants were all enrolled in the Canadian Alliance for Healthy Hearts and Minds (CAHHM) and Prospective Urban and Rural Epidemiology-MIND (PURE-MIND) cohorts.

The researchers used MRI scans to evaluate the participants’ brains. They also set out to analyze the cross-sectional relationship between UPF consumption, body fat, vascular brain injury (VBI), and cognitive performance.

They measured the participants’ habitual UPF intake using a food frequency questionnaire. Intake was expressed as servings based on a 2,000-calorie-per-day diet.

UPFs accounted for an average of 25% of participants’ daily diets. For the individuals with the highest intake, this increased to 45% of everything on their plates, compared to 16% for those who ate the least.

The researchers also noted that UPFs were not just an added part of individual diets. These foods appeared to replace whole foods.

Among people with the highest UPF intake, fruits, vegetables, grains, and other minimally processed foods accounted for 41% of their daily food intake. Among those with the lowest UPF intake, minimally processed foods made up 68% of their daily intake.

When the researchers compared the highest intake to the lowest, they found that those with a higher intake of UPFs had 1.7% more body fat. They also found that people with a higher UPF intake had higher odds of VBI.

“These findings are concerning, but it is important to remember that an association does not prove cause and effect,” said Michelle Routhenstein, Preventive Cardiology Dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“From my perspective as a preventive cardiology dietitian, these findings reinforce the importance of nourishing our blood vessels to support both cardiovascular and brain health.”

Although researchers found no significant differences in cognitive performance, the signs of vascular brain injury observed in the study have been associated with an increased risk of stroke and cognitive decline.

These findings suggest a possible link between UPF intake and signs of brain changes. However, participants were only assessed once and weren’t followed over time. This means the researchers were unable to determine whether UPFs directly caused the changes.

“While we can’t conclude from this study that limiting UPFs would improve brain health, we have decades of literature demonstrating that higher intakes of green leafy vegetables, brightly colored fruits, whole grains, and lean protein sources are good for brain health,” said Suzanne Judd, PhD, Director of the UC College of Medicine’s Center for Public Health, who wasn’t involved in the study.

“So focusing more on what can be brought into the diet rather than what should be eliminated might be a good way to start,” she added.

The preservatives [in UPFs] may have a direct impact on the vascular system, while the loss of antioxidants results in a less robust response to inflammation in the body.

“Additionally, sugar or salt is often added to make the food a bit tastier so people consume it in higher quantities. Over-consumption of food can lead to weight gain and diabetes, which can negatively impact brain health,” Judd said.

Judd told Healthline that it is virtually impossible to completely avoid UPFs. Even some “health” foods, like protein powder and some yogurts, are considered ultra-processed.

It is more important to look at diet overall and emphasize consuming foods as close to their natural state as possible, such as fruits, vegetables, grains, and meats purchased raw or frozen and then cooked at home.

Routhenstein recommended starting simply with one manageable swap rather than trying to overhaul your entire diet.

She said that you should identify an ultra-processed food you eat regularly and consider a more nutrient-rich alternative. It is important to account for convenience, food preferences, budget, and lifestyle so that the change feels realistic and is sustainable over the long term.

“I want people to feel empowered, not fearful, when making food choices,” said Routhenstein. “Healthy eating does not mean having to cook everything from scratch. What matters most is building consistent, nourishing habits that support both heart and brain health.”

#22. Walking, Yoga, or Zumba? Which Exercises Help Relieve Menopause Mood Symptoms

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Physical activity may help support mood and improve quality of life during the menopause transition, according to a study published on October 13 in Menopause, the journal of the Menopause Society.

This review of 12 clinical trials involving over 1,000 people ages 40 to 65 found that both aerobic and mind-body exercises may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms and improved well-being.

It is common for individuals to experience psychological symptoms during menopause, including depression, anxiety, low mood, stress, and difficulty with thinking and memory. These symptoms can have a significant effect on quality of life, with an estimated 1 in 10 women leaving work due to menopause symptoms.

This study found that regular physical activity may help improve certain psychological symptoms and overall quality of life during menopause.

The types of physical activity in the review were broken into two categories: aerobic-based exercise (ABIs) and mind-body activities (MBAs).

The researchers found that ABIs showed the strongest effects on reducing depressive symptoms and improving overall quality of life. This may be particularly relevant during perimenopause when women may be more vulnerable to mood instability due to estrogen fluctuations.

Sherry Ross, MD, board certified OB/GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, pointed out that there are various ways that ABIs can have positive effects.

Ross explained that aerobic exercise may help improve menopausal symptoms through several mechanisms, including the release of endorphins, reduced inflammation, improved cholesterol levels, and better regulation of body temperature and sleep cycles.

MBAs were shown to have a greater effect on improving psychological quality of life. These exercises involve mindfulness, body awareness, and breathwork. They may help improve emotional regulation.

ABIs tended to require fewer sessions. This may mean these types of exercises are easier to fit in, especially if you are facing time or energy constraints. For example, in one trial, participants were instructed to walk for 30 minutes, three times per week. In another trial, participants attended three hour-long Zumba classes per week.

In contrast, one trial using MBAs instructed participants to attend two 75-minute yoga classes each week, and another had participants attend daily one-hour yoga classes.

Stephanie Faubion, MD, Director for the Mayo Clinic Center for Women’s Health and Medical Director for The Menopause Society, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are encouraging.

However, she also cautioned that this was a relatively small body of evidence. “While physical activity is an important component of a healthy lifestyle during midlife, I would be cautious about interpreting these findings to mean that exercise is a treatment for depression related specifically to menopause,” she said.

Research has shown that physical activity can positively affect mental health. Studies have also found that regular exercise can help improve symptoms of depression and anxiety.

Faubion told Healthline that, broadly speaking, regular physical activity may support mood through multiple pathways, including better sleep, improved physical function, and overall well-being.

However, she also noted that this study cannot tell us precisely why the improvements occurred or whether aerobic exercise is superior to other forms of activity.

Faubion said that recommending physical activity during and after menopause extends well beyond mood.

“Physical activity is important for cardiovascular and metabolic health, maintaining muscle mass and physical function, and supporting bone health,” she said.

“These become particularly important at midlife as women experience age- and menopause-related changes in body composition, bone, and cardiometabolic health,” Faubion added.

Ross agreed that physical activity is important for more than just mood and mental health improvement. She also noted that there are other types of exercise beyond ABIs and MBAs that can benefit people during menopause:

“These types of activities help with weight loss or allow women to maintain a healthy weight. If you are not ready for aerobic exercises, stretching and simple exercises help with balance and stability,” Ross said.

Faubion told Healthline that physical activity is an important part of healthy aging and should be encouraged during the menopause transition, but it should not be suggested that women can simply exercise their way out of significant depression or other bothersome menopause symptoms.

“Women experiencing persistent depression, anxiety, or symptoms that interfere with their quality of life should seek evaluation and discuss the full range of evidence-based treatment options with their healthcare clinician,” Faubion said.
